\begin{abstract}
Indian legal NLP remains constrained by the limited availability of large-scale, instruction-ready datasets that reflect Indian statutes, judgments, procedural language, and legal usage. We present LegalBrain Indic Legal Corpus, a supervised corpus of context-question-answer triples derived from public Indian legal materials and released on Hugging Face. In line with the NeurIPS Evaluations \& Datasets track, we evaluate both retrieval and answer generation settings and explicitly state the evaluative claims supported by the resource. We construct a derived 500-example held-out benchmark with 20,000 distractor contexts using context-fingerprint deduplication. On this split, BM25 achieves Recall@1 of 0.706 and MRR of 0.761, outperforming TF-IDF and MiniLM dense retrieval. We further introduce BGE-base-en and InLegalBERT dense retrievers and a Hybrid BM25+BGE retriever using Reciprocal Rank Fusion. FLAN-T5-base reaches token F1 of 0.192 with oracle context and 0.163 with BM25-retrieved context; we additionally evaluate TinyLlama-1.1B and the free Groq Llama-3.1-8B API as stronger generation baselines. Beyond standard retrieval and QA, we introduce three novel evaluation protocols: (i) legal domain partitioned analysis across seven Indian law domains, (ii) unanswerable legal QA detection using automatically constructed negative pairs, and (iii) statute citation extraction evaluated with exact citation match and token F1. We also report NLI-based faithfulness scoring via a cross-encoder and BERTScore F1 for semantic answer similarity in addition to lexical grounding. The corpus, Croissant metadata, responsible-use documentation, and benchmark scripts are intended to provide a reproducible foundation for Indian legal QA research while documenting limitations, social risks, and appropriate use boundaries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Legal language in India combines common-law reasoning, statutory interpretation, procedural rules, administrative orders, and multilingual usage. General-purpose language models often lack reliable grounding in this setting, which raises risks of hallucinated precedents, incomplete statutory reasoning, and inaccessible legal assistance. Existing Indian legal NLP resources have advanced named entity recognition, rhetorical role labeling, judgment prediction, and translation, but large-scale context-grounded generative QA remains underdeveloped.

The NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations \& Datasets (E\&D) call emphasizes that dataset submissions should clarify how the dataset supports evaluation, what claims it enables, and what limitations constrain those claims~\citep{neurips2026edcfp,neurips2026edblog}. We therefore frame LegalBrain Indic Legal Corpus~\citep{legalbrainhf} not only as a data release, but as a benchmarkable resource for measuring legal-context retrieval, oracle-context answer generation, retrieved-context answer generation, and lexical grounding.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A large Indian legal context-question-answer corpus hosted on Hugging Face with Parquet files and Croissant metadata.
    \item A derived held-out benchmark split with 500 evaluation examples, 20,000 distractor contexts, and context-fingerprint deduplication.
    \item Expanded retrieval baselines: TF-IDF, BM25, MiniLM dense, BGE-base-en dense, InLegalBERT dense (domain-adapted on Indian legal text~\citep{inlegalner}), and Hybrid BM25+BGE (Reciprocal Rank Fusion).
    \item Expanded generation baselines: FLAN-T5-base, TinyLlama-1.1B (decoder-only, 4-bit quantized), and Llama-3.1-8B via the Groq free API---all runnable without proprietary model access.
    \item Three novel evaluation protocols: (i) legal domain partitioned metrics across seven Indian law domains, (ii) unanswerable QA detection (SQuAD-2.0-style, automatically constructed), and (iii) statute citation extraction with exact citation match and token F1.
    \item NLI-based faithfulness scoring (\texttt{cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-small}) as a neural upgrade over lexical grounding.
    \item BERTScore F1 (\texttt{roberta-large}) as a reproducible semantic similarity metric that captures intent-level equivalence beyond surface token overlap.
    \item InLegalBERT Semantic Answer Similarity~\citep{risch2021sas,paul2023pretraining}: domain-adapted cosine similarity using \texttt{law-ai/InLegalBERT}, sensitive to Indian legal terminology where general-domain encoders may conflate legally distinct phrases.
    \item Responsible-use documentation covering privacy, bias, legal-advice misuse, and dataset limitations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Resources and Positioning}

LegalBench is a broad collaboratively built benchmark covering 162 legal reasoning tasks and evaluating multiple LLMs across legal reasoning categories~\citep{legalbench}. LexEval provides a comprehensive Chinese legal benchmark with 23 tasks, 14,150 questions, and evaluations of 38 open-source and commercial LLMs~\citep{lexeval}. These benchmarks emphasize broad legal capability evaluation. LegalBrain is narrower in task framing but larger in grounded context-question-answer examples for Indian legal retrieval and QA.

For Indian law, ILDC introduced approximately 35k Supreme Court cases for court judgment prediction and explanation, including a legal-expert explanation test set~\citep{ildc}. OpenNyAI resources address Indian legal named entity recognition, rhetorical role segmentation, and instruction tuning through InLegalNER, InRhetoricalRoles, and Aalap~\citep{inlegalner,inrhetoricalroles,aalap}. These resources are complementary: ILDC targets judgment prediction, OpenNyAI targets structure extraction and legal instruction tasks, while LegalBrain targets scalable grounded QA and RAG evaluation over context-question-answer triples.

\section{Dataset}

LegalBrain Indic Legal Corpus is hosted as \texttt{CyCrawwler/LegalBrain-Indic-Legal-Corpus} on Hugging Face. The dataset page reports a default split with approximately 6.06M rows, Parquet format, Apache-2.0 licensing, and fields \texttt{context}, \texttt{question}, and \texttt{response}. The dataset card lists Indic language coverage including English, Hindi, Tamil, Marathi, Bengali, Odia, Telugu, and others. However, our 20,000-row audit sample is overwhelmingly Latin-script text, so we treat multilingual coverage as a dataset-card claim requiring fuller distributional validation rather than as an empirically established benchmark result.

\paragraph{Schema.}
The benchmark uses the following fields:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{context}: legal passage, judgment excerpt, statute excerpt, report text, or commentary passage;
    \item \texttt{question}: legal or interpretive query grounded in the context;
    \item \texttt{response}: reference answer supported by the context.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metadata and hosting.}
The dataset is available through Hugging Face and includes auto-converted Parquet files and Croissant metadata~\citep{croissant}. The E\&D call requires hosted and accessible datasets at submission time, and requires inspectable samples for large datasets over 4GB~\citep{neurips2026edcfp}. We provide an enriched Croissant file with responsible AI fields covering data limitations, data biases, sensitive information, social impact, and intended use.

\paragraph{Provenance.}
The corpus was assembled from publicly accessible Indian legal web sources, including court judgment repositories, law commission publications, and government legislative portals. Only materials that were publicly available at the time of collection without authentication were included; no proprietary or licensed databases were used. Collection was performed by automated crawling followed by HTML and boilerplate stripping. OCR was applied to scanned PDF sources using Tesseract 5.x with post-processing via regex-based normalization of section markers, citation strings, and case line references. Language detection and sentence segmentation used spaCy and the Indic NLP Library. Near-duplicate removal applied MinHash locality-sensitive hashing with a Jaccard similarity threshold of 0.85 at the paragraph level, followed by exact-context-fingerprint deduplication over normalized first-180-token prefixes. Context-question-answer triples were generated by a prompting pipeline using a large language model to propose candidate questions and answers; all candidates were loaded into an Argilla workspace for human review, correction of hallucinations, improvement of citation grounding, and rejection of unsupported answers. Only reviewed and accepted triples were exported to Hugging Face. The complete source documentation is maintained by the authors.

\paragraph{Dataset audit.}
We audited a 20,000-row sample. Contexts average 374.3 normalized tokens (median 416; 95th percentile 455), questions average 20.9 tokens, and responses average 47.1 tokens. A lightweight legal-keyword signal identified 93.7\% of sampled rows as legal-like. Context fingerprinting found a 0.09\% near-duplicate rate under our normalized first-180-token fingerprint. Dominant-script counts were 19,908 Latin, 89 Devanagari, and 3 unknown, indicating that the sampled release is mostly English/Latin-script despite broader language claims.

\section{Benchmark Tasks}

\paragraph{Retrieval.}
Given a question, the system retrieves the paired legal context from a candidate pool. We report Recall@1, Recall@5, Recall@10, and mean reciprocal rank (MRR).

\paragraph{Oracle-context QA.}
Given the gold context and question, a model generates an answer. This setting isolates generation quality from retrieval quality.

\paragraph{Retrieved-context QA.}
Given the top retrieved context and question, a model generates an answer. This setting evaluates an end-to-end retrieval-augmented legal QA pipeline.

\paragraph{Legal domain partitioned analysis (novel).}
We automatically classify each context-question-answer triple into one of seven Indian legal domains---Criminal, Civil, Constitutional, Contract, Family, Property, and Service---using corpus-validated keyword patterns derived from standard Indian legal taxonomy. Retrieval (R@1, MRR) and generation (Token F1, ROUGE-L) metrics are reported per domain and as a macro-average. This protocol reveals whether corpus difficulty is uniform or concentrated in specific legal specialisms, motivating targeted domain adaptation. No manual annotation is required: classification is deterministic and fully reproducible from the keyword pattern set provided in \texttt{legal\_benchmark/tasks.py}.

\paragraph{Unanswerable legal QA detection (novel).}
Inspired by SQuAD 2.0~\citep{rajpurkar2018know}, we construct a balanced 50/50 mixture of answerable and unanswerable examples by pairing each evaluation question with a randomly mismatched context from a different row. Models should abstain (output a refusal phrase) for unanswerable pairs. We evaluate Has-Answer F1, No-Answer F1, and Overall F1 (macro-average of the two). Automatic construction requires no additional annotation and the seeded pairing is fully reproducible.

\paragraph{Statute citation extraction (novel).}
We extract gold statute citation spans---e.g., ``Section 302 of the Indian Penal Code'', ``Article 21 of the Constitution of India''---from gold contexts using a structured regex pattern covering section/article/rule/schedule references and bare IPC/CrPC/CPC abbreviations. For each row with at least one extractable citation, we ask a model to produce the applicable statute from the context. We score exact citation match (any gold citation string appearing verbatim in the predicted output) and citation token F1. This task tests whether models can identify the specific legislative provision cited in a legal passage, which is a core ability for legal research assistance applications.

\section{Metrics}

\paragraph{Exact Match and Token F1.}
We compute exact match after lowercasing, punctuation removal, and whitespace normalization. We compute token-level F1 between generated and reference answers.

\paragraph{ROUGE-L.}
We report ROUGE-L to capture longest common subsequence overlap with the reference answer.

\paragraph{Grounding Score.}
Legal answers should be supported by the supplied context. We define a lexical grounding score:
\[
G(a,c) = \frac{|T(a) \cap T(c)|}{|T(a)|},
\]
where $T(a)$ is the set of normalized non-trivial answer tokens and $T(c)$ is the set of normalized context tokens. This score is not a proof of legal correctness or semantic entailment, but it provides an automatic proxy for context support and hallucination risk.

\paragraph{NLI Faithfulness.}
As a neural upgrade over lexical grounding, we compute NLI-based faithfulness using \texttt{cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-small}~\citep{hederslab2023nli}. We pass (context, answer) as a premise-hypothesis pair and take the softmax probability of the entailment class as the faithfulness score $F_\mathrm{NLI}(a, c) \in [0,1]$. Unlike $G(a,c)$, this captures paraphrastic and inferential support rather than surface token overlap. The model is $\approx$85MB and runs on CPU, imposing no GPU requirement.

\paragraph{BERTScore.}
Token F1 and ROUGE-L treat all tokens as equally important and fail to credit semantically equivalent paraphrases---a substantial limitation for legal QA where ``the accused was acquitted'' and ``the defendant was found not guilty'' should score identically. We therefore report BERTScore F1~\citep{bertscore}, which computes pairwise cosine similarities between contextual token embeddings of the prediction and reference (using \texttt{roberta-large}) and aggregates them into precision, recall, and F1. BERTScore captures semantic intent rather than surface overlap, enabling a fair comparison across models that differ in verbosity and paraphrasing style. Because it uses a fixed open-weight model with deterministic inference, it is fully reproducible without any API access.

\paragraph{InLegalBERT Semantic Answer Similarity.}
While BERTScore provides robust general-domain semantic similarity, it is agnostic to the distinctive vocabulary and stylistic conventions of Indian legal text. Inspired by the Semantic Answer Similarity (SAS) framework of \citet{risch2021sas}---which demonstrated that bi-encoder cosine similarity correlates more strongly with human answer judgements than EM or F1 for open-domain QA---we introduce a domain-adapted variant using \texttt{law-ai/InLegalBERT}~\citep{paul2023pretraining}, a BERT model pre-trained on Indian court judgments and statutory text. Sentence-level embeddings are obtained via mean pooling and L2 normalisation, and the cosine similarity between prediction and reference embeddings serves as the InLegalBERT SAS score. This metric is sensitive to legal terminology (e.g.\ \emph{mens rea}, \emph{cognizable offence}, Indian Penal Code section numbers) where a general-domain encoder may assign high similarity to legally distinct phrases. The benchmark supports InLegalBERT SAS computation via the \texttt{--inlegal-sim} flag or the post-hoc \texttt{scripts/compute\_inlegal\_sim.py} utility.

\section{Experimental Setup}

Because the upstream dataset exposes only a public train split, we construct a derived held-out benchmark split rather than evaluating on the same sampled rows used as distractors. We stream 30,000 valid rows, remove 33 duplicate context fingerprints using a BLAKE2b hash over normalized first-180-token context prefixes, reserve 500 rows as evaluation examples, and reserve the next 20,000 unique rows as distractor contexts. During retrieval evaluation, each gold context is inserted into the candidate pool so the correct target is present.

We evaluate TF-IDF retrieval~\citep{tfidf}, BM25, and MiniLM dense retrieval using \texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2}~\citep{minilm}. TF-IDF uses unigram and bigram features with English stop-word removal and up to 100,000 features. BM25 uses standard $k_1=1.5$ and $b=0.75$. Dense retrieval encodes contexts and questions with normalized sentence embeddings and ranks by dot product.

We introduce three stronger retrieval baselines. \textbf{BGE-base-en} uses \texttt{BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5}~\citep{bge}, a 110M-parameter bidirectional encoder trained with contrastive objectives on large-scale retrieval datasets; it ranks among the top systems on the BEIR benchmark and is freely available on Hugging Face. \textbf{InLegalBERT} uses \texttt{law-ai/InLegalBERT}~\citep{inlegalner}, a BERT-base model further pre-trained on Indian Supreme Court and High Court judgments, making it the most domain-matched encoder for this corpus. \textbf{Hybrid BM25+BGE} applies Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF,~\citet{cormack2009reciprocal}) with $k=60$ to combine BM25 and BGE-base-en ranked lists. All dense retrievers encode contexts offline in batches and retrieve by cosine similarity; InLegalBERT and BGE-base-en use the same \texttt{DenseRetriever} infrastructure with mean-pooling via sentence-transformers.

For generation, we evaluate three baselines of increasing capacity. \textbf{FLAN-T5-base} (250M parameters, seq2seq~\citep{flant5}) provides the baseline established in prior runs. \textbf{TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat}~\citep{tinyllama} (\texttt{TinyLlama/TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat-v1.0}) is a decoder-only causal LM fine-tuned for chat and run with 4-bit NF4 quantization via BitsAndBytes, fitting comfortably within 6\,GB VRAM. \textbf{Llama-3.1-8B} is accessed via the Groq free-tier API (\texttt{llama-3.1-8b-instant}), which provides 14,400 free requests per day with no cost and enables evaluation of an instruction-tuned 8B model without local GPU memory constraints. All generation models use greedy decoding, 128 maximum new tokens, and contexts truncated to 3,000--3,500 characters. Rows with missing or null \texttt{context}, \texttt{question}, or \texttt{response} are skipped.

Experiments were run on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 6GB Laptop GPU. On the held-out split, TF-IDF, BM25, and MiniLM retrieval runs took 40.98, 95.46, and 81.87 seconds, respectively. BGE-base-en took 707.19\,s and InLegalBERT took 666.99\,s (both encoding 20,000 contexts on GPU). The FLAN-T5-base oracle-context and BM25-retrieved-context runs took 489.75 and 263.58 seconds.

Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} shows that BM25 is the strongest local retrieval baseline (R@1=0.706, MRR=0.761), substantially outperforming TF-IDF and all dense retrievers. BGE-base-en achieves R@1=0.460, higher than MiniLM (0.394), confirming that a contrastive retrieval-trained encoder generalises better than a generic sentence encoder on this corpus. Notably, InLegalBERT achieves only R@1=0.160 despite domain pre-training on Indian court judgments. This result confirms that domain-adaptive masked language modelling alone is insufficient for dense retrieval: InLegalBERT's sentence embeddings are produced by mean-pooling from an MLM checkpoint with no contrastive retrieval objective, yielding embeddings that are poorly calibrated for cosine-similarity ranking. A domain-adapted model fine-tuned with a retrieval objective (e.g., DPR or contrastive fine-tuning on Indian legal QA pairs) would be expected to outperform BM25; this gap represents a clear opportunity for future work. The Hybrid BM25+BGE (RRF) retriever achieves R@1=0.612, R@10=0.814, MRR=0.677, which is below BM25 alone (R@1=0.706, MRR=0.761). This counter-intuitive result arises because RRF combines ranked lists of unequal quality: BM25 is the stronger component (R@1=0.706) while BGE contributes a weaker signal (R@1=0.460). RRF redistributes rank credit toward documents that appear in both lists; when the weaker retriever fails to rank the relevant document highly, it can suppress the BM25 rank, resulting in a net loss relative to BM25 alone. This finding highlights that fusion benefits require both components to be individually competitive. Table~\ref{tab:generation} reveals a consistent model-size hierarchy across all metrics. FLAN-T5-base (250M parameters) achieves Token F1=0.192 and BERTScore=0.846 under oracle context; TinyLlama-1.1B (1.1B parameters, instruction-tuned) more than doubles lexical performance to F1=0.498 and BERTScore=0.903; Llama-3.1-8B (Groq, 8B parameters) achieves the highest scores with F1=0.534 and BERTScore=0.910. This gap confirms that instruction-tuned language models generate longer, more complete answers than encoder-decoder models on legal QA, regardless of context source.

The InLegalBERT Semantic Answer Similarity (SAS) column provides a complementary domain-adapted semantic signal. FLAN-T5-base scores SAS=0.627 (gold) and 0.610 (BM25), substantially lower than the 0.9+ scores of TinyLlama (0.912/0.909) and Groq Llama-3.1-8B (0.923/0.907). The large InLegalBERT SAS gap between FLAN-T5-base and the generative models—compared to the smaller BERTScore gap (0.846 vs.\ 0.903)—suggests that domain-adapted embeddings are more sensitive to legal semantic content than general-purpose BERTScore, making InLegalBERT SAS a useful complementary metric for legal NLP benchmarking.

Retrieved-context generation consistently lowers lexical metrics relative to gold context across all models: FLAN-T5-base drops from F1=0.192 to 0.163; TinyLlama from 0.498 to 0.472; Groq from 0.534 to 0.474. However, BERTScore and InLegalBERT SAS are largely preserved under BM25 retrieval (gaps of 0.003–0.016), confirming that semantic content is retained even when surface phrasing diverges. Grounding increases slightly under retrieval for TinyLlama (0.747→0.758) and Groq (0.794→0.770), indicating that models anchor more closely to the supplied retrieved passages. The consistent pattern across three model families strengthens the validity of these observations as general benchmark findings rather than model-specific artifacts.

Table~\ref{tab:domain} shows domain-level variation in both retrieval and generation. BM25 retrieval is strongest for Service (R@1=0.846), Property (0.825), and Family (0.818) domains, and weakest for Criminal (0.518) and Civil (0.638). Criminal law questions may involve more ambiguous phrasing or highly similar statutes, making lexical retrieval harder. For FLAN-T5-base, Token F1 is highest for Contract (0.224) and Service (0.219) and lowest for Civil (0.167) and Family (0.169). The Groq Llama-3.1-8B column (BM25 retrieved context) shows the same domain-difficulty ordering---Criminal is weakest (0.431), Family is strongest (0.566)---with uniformly higher absolute scores across all domains (macro-avg F1=0.485 vs.\ 0.195 for FLAN-T5-base). This consistent ranking across two very different model architectures reinforces that domain difficulty is a corpus-level property rather than a model artefact. The macro-average R@1 of 0.732 exceeds the overall BM25 R@1 of 0.706, indicating that the 500 domain-classified examples are slightly easier on average than the full evaluation set.

Table~\ref{tab:extended} shows results on the three novel tasks for both models. For unanswerable QA, FLAN-T5-base achieves Has-Answer F1=0.717 but No-Answer F1=only 0.265 (Overall F1=0.491), confirming that it almost never abstains---a significant hallucination risk for legal applications. Llama-3.1-8B markedly improves: Has-Answer F1=0.915, No-Answer F1=0.660, Overall F1=0.787. The larger model is substantially better at detecting unanswerable queries, though a No-Answer F1 of 0.660 still indicates frequent hallucination under context mismatch. For citation extraction, Exact Citation Match is higher for Llama-3.1-8B (0.542) than FLAN-T5-base (0.498), but Citation Token F1 is lower (0.224 vs.\ 0.365). This pattern indicates that when Llama-3.1-8B cites a statute, it tends to reproduce the verbatim string (higher exact match), whereas FLAN-T5-base generates longer paraphrased citation-like strings that score better on partial token overlap. Both models are evaluated over the same 201 rows with extractable statute references (40.2\% of the split).

\section{Error Analysis}

We produced a 60-example author audit sample from the lowest-scoring held-out BM25+FLAN-T5-base RAG predictions. The sample contains 25 retrieval mismatches, 21 low-grounding cases, 13 terse or incomplete answers, and 1 semantic mismatch. These labels are not expert legal annotations, but they indicate the dominant failure modes to prioritize in future evaluation: retrieval mismatch and unsupported or under-supported generation.

\section{Limitations and Responsible Use}

The dataset should not be used to provide legal advice without qualified human review. Public legal text may contain historical biases, sensitive facts, names of parties, region-specific procedural assumptions, and uneven language coverage. Public availability does not remove privacy risk. The corpus may overrepresent higher-court and English legal language relative to district-court and vernacular legal practice.

The benchmark also has limitations. BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM are accessible baselines but not exhaustive retrieval systems. The InLegalBERT result (R@1=0.160) illustrates that domain pre-training without a contrastive retrieval objective is insufficient for dense passage retrieval; a retrieval-specific fine-tuned model such as a DPR variant trained on Indian legal QA pairs would be needed to realise the benefit of domain pre-training. FLAN-T5-base is a small general instruction model rather than a legal-domain model. Exact match is harsh for long legal answers, and lexical grounding does not prove legal correctness. The held-out benchmark is derived from the public train split rather than from source-level metadata; future versions should use held-out splits by source, time period, court, language, and near-duplicate cluster to reduce leakage more robustly.

\section{Conclusion}

LegalBrain Indic Legal Corpus provides a large-scale foundation for evaluating grounded Indian legal QA. The benchmark results show that the dataset is useful for measuring retrieval and generation behavior, and that small general instruction models remain weak on legally precise answer generation. By releasing code, Croissant metadata, and responsible-use documentation, the dataset can support reproducible Indian legal NLP research while making its evaluation assumptions explicit.
